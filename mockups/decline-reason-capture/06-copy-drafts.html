Reading notes

What the wording does to the answer

Second-person blame changes the distribution, not just the tone. “They’re overasking” and “Bad value for my trade partner” both ask the user to render a verdict on a named person they will see again. Round‑2’s central finding about closed leagues is that these are repeated relationships — the reason LinkedIn hides declines is not squeamishness, it is that the two parties keep meeting. We are not showing Brett the answer, but the user does not fully believe that, and an option that reads as an accusation will be under-selected relative to one that reads as a preference. First-person framing (“Not enough coming back”, “Not moving Smith”) sidesteps it entirely.

“Ok Value” and “Good Value” are not reasons. They describe the trade, not the objection. A user who taps “Good Value” has told us the price is fine and told us nothing about what to change — and since “the price is fine” is precisely the case where the engine most needs to know what else is wrong, those two rows spend the two most valuable slots on the least actionable answers.

Length is a taxonomy constraint. Three chips must fit one row at 390 pt, and the type floor is 11 px with no exceptions. That caps a chip at roughly 16–18 characters before it wraps — which is why the shipping recommendation forks “Wrong players” into named assets on a second tap rather than trying to fit “Don’t want James Cook” into a third of a row.

Do not promise learning. “This trains the engine” over-claims: reasons route the next draft, they do not update player values, because stated reasons are confabulated (de Corte et al., Health Economics 2021 — people genuinely do not know why they chose what they chose). The receipt should describe the concrete next step (“we’ll ask Brett for more”), which is both true and more reassuring than a claim about a model.

Recommended copy

Column 2 (plain-spoken) for the options, column 4’s brevity for the heading. Plain-spoken is the only voice that is simultaneously first-person, non-accusatory and unambiguous, and it is the only one whose chips survive being read by someone who is mildly annoyed and about to leave the screen. Concise-neutral is a support form; fantasy-native is fun but makes the user say something about a leaguemate.

Concretely, for the recommended one-tap row:

  • Row caption: “Or pass because” — frames the chips as alternative pass buttons, not a survey.
  • Chips: Not enough back · Wrong players · Not right now.
  • Fork heading: “Which one?” with the two real names and “Neither — wrong shape”.
  • Overflow: “Not Brett right now” and “Too good — I’d be robbing him”.
  • Receipt: “Same players — we’ll ask Brett for more.” Never “thanks, this trains the engine.”
  • Privacy: “Brett never sees any of this.” Say it once, on first exposure only — repeating it every pass makes the pass feel consequential, which is the opposite of the goal.
