Decline reason capture

Five genuinely different ways to learn why a user hit the x on a suggested trade — plus four copy voices for the same taxonomy. The check and x stay exactly as they are in every approach; none of these designs changes what a pass is. What varies is where the question lives, how many taps it costs, and how easy it is to walk away from.

Start here: the three things that decide this

1. The pass must stay free

The dismissed-after-viewed signal is graded the only trustworthy negative in the whole implicit-feedback ladder (round‑2 §1.5: confidence 3–5, versus 0.5–1 for scrolled-past and ~0 for never-viewed). It is trustworthy because exposure is controlled — the user demonstrably saw it and said no. Any design that makes people pass less does not just cost us reasons, it destroys the asset we were trying to enrich.

2. Two axes, not one list

The operator’s instinct is right and the drafted list cannot carry it. “Price” and “players” are independent, and the cell the flat list cannot express — the price is fine, I just don’t want this guy — is the single most actionable answer available, because it names a blocker. See the 3×3 grid.

3. Reasons route; they don’t train

Stated reasons are confabulated more often than they are dishonest (de Corte et al., Health Economics 2021 — people genuinely do not know why they chose what they chose). So the payoff is routing the next draft, never updating player values. Every reason below therefore has to earn its slot by mapping to a distinct engine action. If two options route to the same place, one of them is decoration.

What’s already there, and why it matters for this design. A pass today is one tap on the icon-only x in the card’s disposition row (TradeCard.tsx, the #169 “card frame C” row; testIDs trades.pass‑btn / trades.like‑btn), or a left swipe — the host wires both to the same advance(), so the deck advance, the haptic and the API call are identical on either path.

Three shipped pieces every approach here reuses rather than reinvents. (a) A 5‑second undo window: the pass is held client-side and the “Passed · Undo” toast is already the post-pass surface. (b) The F1 signal spine behind deck.signal_v2 — /api/trades/swipe already accepts impression_id, dwell_ms, detail_expanded, calc_opened, which is the exposure control a reason needs to be worth anything. (c) The bad-trade flag (feedback #85), which already routes through advance('pass') and fires trade_flagged — a reason taxonomy should absorb it, not sit beside it.

The quiet bug this fixes. Every pass today writes an Elo signal treating the give side as the winner (record_trade_signal, k = trade_k_pass). That is correct for exactly one reason — “not enough coming back.” When the real reason is “I don’t want this player” or “not this manager,” we are teaching the board a value lesson the user never intended. Reason capture is not only a routing feature; it is a gate on a corrupting write.

Recommendation

Ship B, instrumented as E, with D’s receipt as the fallback

Ship approach B — reason as action. It is the only shape where a labelled pass and an unlabelled pass cost the same single tap, which means it is the only shape that cannot damage the thing we most need to protect. The strongest negative signal we collect is the dismissal itself, and it is strong only because the card was demonstrably viewed and explicitly rejected; a design that adds a toll booth to the x buys reasons with the very data it is enriching. B has no toll booth. The x is untouched, so the unlabelled pass rate cannot regress — the worst case is that nobody taps the chips and we are exactly where we started.

Spend the one extra tap only on the blocker fork. “Wrong players” is the single branch where a second tap changes which engine action fires, because naming which asset is what turns an unlabelled negative into an identified blocker. That is the whole mechanism the counteroffer loop runs on: a rejection of a package differing by exactly one asset tells you that asset was the blocker (Baarslag et al., AAMAS 2016), while a multi-asset revision teaches nothing. Everywhere else, a second tap buys tone, not routing — so it doesn’t get one.

Demote, don’t delete, the rare codes. Three chips is the budget (Kleinerman, RecSys 2018, capping concrete items at three via Pu & Chen), so partner and value_overpay_them live in the card overflow. Both are low-frequency; value_overpay_them is nonetheless the only signal that can tell us our valuation errs in the user’s favour, so it must exist somewhere — and this is why approach E, which cannot reach it at all, is not the recommendation.

Borrow E’s discipline for the first release. Ship B behind a flag with the full event pair below, and treat the first four weeks as measurement: the question is not “did engagement go up” but “did passes per session stay flat, and is the reason distribution stable enough to route on.” If the chip row proves to be visual weight nobody uses, B degrades to today’s card by turning one flag off.

Keep D in the drawer. If the permanent chip row is rejected on density grounds — a fair objection, the card is already tall — D delivers the same taxonomy with no per-card chrome, at the cost of a receipt strip and one extra tap. It is the only approach that allows a late answer, which may matter more than it looks.

Copy: the plain-spoken column. First-person, non-accusatory, unambiguous — see the copy drafts for why “They’re overasking” and “Bad value for my trade partner” will be under-selected in a league you cannot leave.

What would change my mind

If the operator’s real goal is measuring the taxonomy rather than routing each revision — i.e. “I want to know what fraction of passes are price versus players” — then E is the better answer and B is over-built. Sampling gets a distribution at a fraction of the design risk. The two goals look identical when written down and diverge completely in what they need: routing needs per-card coverage, measurement needs an unbiased sample. Worth settling that before anyone builds.

If the deck is short enough that most users see fewer than ~5 cards per session, the permanent chip row becomes a large share of the screen for very few passes, and D’s post-hoc strip is the better trade.

The load-bearing consequence

Five of the eight codes should suppress the Elo write that a pass fires today. That is not a side effect of this feature — it may be the largest single thing it does. Every “I don’t want this player” pass currently teaches the ranking board that the give-side beat the receive-side, which is a value claim the user never made. Any build of this must decide the suppression behaviour explicitly and put it behind its own flag, because it changes ranking math and is therefore squarely on the wrong side of the express-lane bright line.

Contract deltas this implies (not just events)

POST /api/trades/swipe gains three optional body fields alongside the existing F1 set — pass_reason, blocker_asset_id, blocker_asset_kind. Absent → exactly today’s behaviour, so old binaries are unaffected. That is an API-contract change and a docs/api-reference.md row.

Three names join ALLOWED_CLIENT_EVENTS in backend/analytics_taxonomy.py, each with its own ALLOWED_PROPS frozenset covering every property above. Unknown props are stripped and counted, so a missing entry fails silently — write the frozensets in the same change, not after.

The trade_flagged bad-trade flag (feedback #85) overlaps this taxonomy and should be reconciled rather than left parallel — “the engine got this one wrong” is arguably fit plus an operator-review marker.

What I’d want measured before calling it

Guardrails first, then the reason distribution

The primary read is a guardrail, not a lift. Everything here is optional-by-construction, so the interesting question is whether it did any harm.

  • Passes per active session, and pass rate among viewed cards. The kill criterion. If either falls, turn the flag off — a labelled pass is worth less than an unlabelled one that still happens.
  • Undo rate on passes. A rise means the chips are being mis-tapped, which would poison the taxonomy at the same time as the disposition.
  • Answer rate = selected / prompted, split by capture_ui. Anything under ~15% on an always-on shape means the row is chrome.
  • Reason distribution and its stability week over week. The whole point. If it is 90% one code, the taxonomy is not earning seven slots.
  • Blocker-fork completion rate — of users who tap “Wrong players,” what share name an asset. This is the single number that justifies the one extra tap.
  • Revision acceptance: of declines routed to Change assets or Revise package, what share of the next suggestion to that pair gets a like. Reasons only pay off here.

How to read it at this N. Not with a classical fixed-horizon A/B test: at FTF’s volume a small conversion delta needs thousands per arm, and league-level interference (one trade changes every roster) makes between-user arms leaky anyway. Use a within-user crossover — alternate the flag by week per user, compare each user against themselves — or Bayesian monitoring with a weak prior at effective N ≈ 50, shipping at P(better) ≥ 95% and accepting that only large effects are visible. That is a feature: at this scale an experiment that cannot see less than a ~30% change filters for the levers worth building.

One prerequisite that is not optional. Viewport-visibility logging must be live before this ships, not with it. A reason attached to a card we cannot prove was examined is worth roughly nothing, and dwell_ms is what separates a strong negative from exposure bias laundered into a label.

Chalkline notes

Gaps hit while building this

No spec for a multi-select or single-select chip group. components.md covers Badges & chips as display constructions (position, tier, rookie) and the target-picker’s removable chips, but there is no selectable-option chip — the thing every approach here needs. I used: 1px --line-strong border, --ink-1/--ink-0 fill, radius --r-sm, min-height 44, and selected = ice border + --ink-3 fill + a 3px ice tick inside the chip. If any of this ships, that belongs in components.md as a named component rather than being re-derived per feature.

No interactive-toast spec. The Toast component is specced as a passive receipt with an optional single action; approach E puts three tap targets and a close control inside one. Either that is a new component (“PromptToast”) or E is out of spec — worth an explicit call rather than a quiet extension.

Chip length vs the 11px type floor. Three chips across 390 pt with a 44 pt touch floor and no text below 11 px caps a chip at roughly 16–18 characters. That is a hard taxonomy constraint, not a copy preference, and it is why the recommended shape forks rather than fitting “Don’t want James Cook” into a third of a row. Under a11y.text_scaling at ×2.0 the row must be allowed to wrap to two lines rather than truncate.

Semantic colour on a non-destructive control. The reason chips complete a pass, so they are arguably --neg-family actions; I kept them neutral (--line-strong border, chalk-dim text) and reserved --neg for the x itself, so the row reads as quieter than the primary control rather than as three more red buttons. Worth confirming that reading — the alternative (all four pass affordances in --neg) is defensible and looks very different.

Not a gap, but noted: the “Passed · Undo” toast, the 5‑second hold and the F1 signal fields all already exist, so none of these approaches needs new plumbing to know that a pass happened — only why.
