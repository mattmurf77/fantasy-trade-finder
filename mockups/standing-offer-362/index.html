#362 — Standing offer: broaden a liked 1-for-1

“Idea on the cycling thru one player for one pick. If you accept hey I’m willing to trade Malik Willis for a 2028 1st maybe you just add a label so the rest of the league knows he’s willing to trade for a first. Or a pop up after you say yes to that trade that’s like hey I’ll sell Willis for a 27 or 28 first but not 29 or maybe like a first from any of these rosters but not xyz” — jonbonjourvi, TradesHome, v1.15.0, 2026-08-19

Operator framing (2026-08-19): when a user likes a 1-for-1 where they receive a first, prompt them to select all other teams they’d take a first from and all other years they’d accept. Team selection and year selection are independent. Completing the flow prioritizes showing that card to every user on a selected team.

The one-line design question

Today a “like” is stored as one exact package — (give_ids, receive_ids) — and a league-mate only sees the “They’re interested” pill when their deck happens to generate the exact mirror. This item asks the like to become a generalized standing offer: one player out, one asset class in (a 1st), across a chosen year set × a chosen team set. The design work is the prompt that collects those two sets without turning a swipe into a form.

The plumbing already exists. load_recent_league_likes() (backend/database.py:4425) reads league-mates’ likes from the last 90 days, and _inject_likes_you_cards() (backend/server.py:2807) boosts/pins the mirror into their deck with the “They’re interested” pill (TradeCard.tsx:363), capped at 3 injections. #362 widens the match rule feeding that same injector — it does not need a new surface on the receiving end.

Two independent multi-selects, one screen. Years is a 3-pill row; teams is a two-up checkbox grid. Nothing is nested inside anything else, which is the operator’s constraint made literal — a matrix picker (this team × these years) would be the wrong shape and four times the taps.

Prefilled from the card you just liked. 2028 and @qa_l1_member_05 arrive checked and captioned FROM THIS OFFER. 2027 and two teams are shown pre-checked here only to illustrate a mid-edit state; the shipped default is source-only — see the open question in §6.

The trailing numbers earn the row. Each team shows which of your selected years it actually still owns a 1st in. @zeroRB_zealot shows “—”: they have no first left to give, so checking them broadcasts into a vacuum. This is the difference between a picker and a guess, and it’s free — GET /api/league/picks already serves it.

Skipping is a first-class exit. “Just this one trade” dismisses with today’s exact behavior: the like is already committed by the time this sheet appears. The sheet can never cost you the like.

The label Jon asked for, scoped honestly. ◈ Open to 1sts · ’27–’28 is a chip on the card, not a badge on the player everywhere in the app. It says what you committed to and in which years, and it appears on every card in your deck that matches the standing offer.

Why not a global player badge. A permanent “Willis: open to 1sts” marker outlives the intent that created it — rosters change, the QB you were shopping becomes the QB you need. The chip is bound to the offer record and expires with it.

The toast names the count and the exit. “4 teams” is the only number that matters, and the manage location is stated once so the offer never becomes something the user can’t find or revoke.

Reuses the ice accent correctly — the chip is an action-state marker on your own commitment. The counterparty-side pill (§4) stays flare, matching the shipped “They’re interested” treatment.

No new surface on this side. This is the shipped likes-you card (_inject_likes_you_cards + the flare “They’re interested” pill), reached through a wider match rule. That is the whole reason this item is small: the receiving experience already exists and already has a boost/pin path and a 3-injection cap.

One line of new copy. “Why you’re seeing this” replaces the generic fair-value idea text when the card came from a standing offer, and it names the offer in the sender’s own terms. Without it, a boosted card is indistinguishable from a lucky generation.

It must not leak the un-selected teams. The recipient learns that they were selected — never who else was, and never who was excluded. Jon’s “but not xyz” framing is a private negative; surfacing it would start fights.

Cap interaction, unresolved. Standing-offer cards and organic likes-you cards compete for the same 3 injection slots. Which wins when a league gets chatty is an open question (§6).

Every broadcast needs a revoke. A standing offer is the first thing in FTF that puts your intent in front of other people without a further tap from you. It has to be listable, editable, and killable from one place, or it becomes a thing users are afraid to use.

Expiry is shown, not hidden. The existing likes-you window is 90 days (load_recent_league_likes). A standing offer is a louder signal than a private like and should almost certainly expire faster — 21 days is drawn here as a starting proposal, not a decision (§6).

Placement. Matches is where cross-user trade state already lives, so this is a section there rather than a new tab. It is not in Settings — this is content, not configuration.

§6 · What this needs before it can be built

Not as simple as it looks — three real decisions

QuestionWhy it isn’t obviousProposal
Default selection Pre-checking every team maximizes reach and is the “broadcast” reading of the ask — but it makes the sheet a confirm, and users who tap through blast the whole league by accident. Pre-checking only the source team makes it a real choice but buries the value behind 4+ taps. Source-only checked, with a prominent All affordance per group. The CTA count (“Broadcast to 1 team”) makes an unedited tap-through visibly weak, which is the right nudge.
Asset class granularity

An offer that says “any 1st” will generate cards the sender would refuse — which trains them to stop trusting the pill.
This premise is false — see the correction below.		 Bound the offer by the value of the pick in the card that created it, ±1 ladder tier. The sender never sees the math; they see the years and teams they chose. Needs an explicit call before build.
Rejected. No value gate is built. The offer is bounded by round, by the user’s own season and team selection, and by the existing D-055 floor.
Injection cap _inject_likes_you_cards caps at 3 per deck. In a 12-team league where standing offers catch on, a single user could be targeted by six of them and the cap silently drops the rest — with no signal to sender or recipient that it happened. Keep the cap; add a reserved split (e.g. 2 organic + 1 standing-offer) and log the drop so the decision is measurable rather than invisible.
Correction — 2026-08-19, after the build spec was written. The “asset class granularity” row above is wrong on its facts and the ±1-ladder-tier proposal was rejected. It is left in place, struck through, rather than deleted: this lab is read for its reasoning, and a silently-rewritten premise teaches nothing.

What the row got wrong. FTF does not price a rebuilder’s 2027 1st differently from a contender’s. Two shipped decisions make every first in a league carry exactly the same engine value:
• Slot is not a pricing input. pick_pool_value prices every league pick at the generic ladder’s Mid rung of its round (backend/pick_values.py:264-286). D-090 re-examined this and did not overturn it; per-slot pricing is logged unbuilt as Q-023. Slot labels are display-only.
• Year is not a pricing input for firsts. D-079 set round-1 year decay to 1.00 (backend/pick_values.py:159-163, knob pick_year_decay_r1). A 2029 1st prices identically to a 2026 1st — that was the whole point of D-079.

Why that kills the proposal. A ±1-tier band over a set of identical values admits everything. There is no per-(team, year) first value for the band to be relative to. It would be code that looks like a safeguard and does nothing.

What bounds the offer instead: the round (fixed to the originating pick’s round, parsed free from the pick id); the seasons and teams the user picks — Jon’s “but not xyz” is a hand-written roster-quality filter, and a better instrument than any value band we could infer; and the pre-existing D-055 user-gain floor on the receiving side, which standing-offer cards inherit for free because they run through the same injector loop. The one real exposure — a market_slots user, for whom season does spread value — is exactly what D-055 was built to catch.

Revisit only if Q-023 (per-slot pick pricing) is ever built. That, not #362, is where a value band would belong. Ruling recorded as D-362-1; build contract in docs/feedback/items/362-standing-offer/.

Trigger condition — narrower than “any 1-for-1”

The prompt should fire only when all of these hold, or it becomes a nag on a swipe surface whose entire value is speed:

  • The like was a 1-for-1, and
  • the received asset is a pick (the generalizable side — “any 1st” means something; “any Tyler Warren” does not), and
  • the user has no active standing offer for that player, and
  • they haven’t dismissed the prompt twice this session.
Scope note. Jon’s “or a pop up after you say yes” already anticipates the interruption cost. The prompt is the feature; getting its trigger wrong is the way this ships and then gets flagged as annoying two weeks later.
