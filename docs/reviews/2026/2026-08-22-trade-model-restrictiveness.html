Why the deck runs dry

The trade model isn’t too strict in one place. It’s cautious in about twenty places at once, and it stops shopping after six of your eleven leaguemates. This is what the production data says, why it happens, and what to change first.

10,560 served cards

Decisions

Source

01

The short answer

You are right, but not for the reason you’d expect. The deck isn’t small — a typical one holds 25 cards. The problem is that those 25 cards are drawn from a very shallow well, and the well is shallow for three separate reasons that stack on top of each other.

1. The engine stops shopping after six trade partners. It works through your leaguemates one at a time and quits once it has collected enough cards. In an eleven-opponent league it typically quits at six. The other five are never even considered — not rejected, never looked at.

2. For each partner it only considers a third of their roster. Before any fairness check runs, the engine narrows each side down to twelve assets. On a normal 33-player dynasty roster that means 64% of it is invisible for that matchup.

3. On the majority of cards, “good for both teams” is mathematically impossible to satisfy. When your leaguemate hasn’t ranked players, both sides of the trade get priced with the exact same numbers. One side’s gain is then, by definition, the other side’s loss. So the engine can’t look for mutual gain — it substitutes a different rule: you win, but not by too much.

The good news is at the end: a different engine is already running in production alongside the current one, and users are liking its trades roughly three times as often.

Read the two red bars together. The famous fairness rules cut the field to 1,827. Then a step nobody has ever audited — the shortlist that decides which surviving trades are “too similar to each other” — throws away 91% of what was left. That single step removes more defensible trades than any fairness rule in the system.

The five real causes

Cause 1

It stops after six partners

The engine loops through leaguemates and quits once it has banked enough cards. With eight cards per partner and a budget of forty-eight, it quits at partner six. Partners seven through eleven are never generated against at all — so their trades appear in no rejection log, because they were never considered.

global_target = max(30, max_per_opponent × 6)

Cause 2

It only looks at 12 assets a side

For each partner the engine shortlists twelve assets per team — the ones you and they disagree about most in raw value. Everything else is unreachable. On a 33-asset roster that hides two-thirds of the team, and the hidden part includes every player you agree on but have opposite needs at.

v3_pool_size = 12

Cause 3

Mutual gain can’t be computed for most cards

When the other manager hasn’t ranked players — about 85% of served cards — both sides get priced with one set of numbers. Your gain is then exactly their loss. The engine cannot ask “do both teams win?”, so it asks “does the viewer win, and by less than double?” instead. The fairness slider isn’t a quality bar there; it’s a cap on how badly the other manager is being asked to lose.

Cause 4

Twenty rules that mostly say the same thing

Four separate rules all punish “one big player for several smaller ones,” using four different formulas on three different value scales. 97.6% of rejections are made by two or more rules at once. That’s why loosening any single knob produces almost nothing — and why the last loosening experiment looked like a failure.

Cause 5

Whole trade shapes are impossible to build

A 3-for-1 consolidation cannot be generated on the main path — a hard rule forbids sides differing by more than one player. And on the 85% path, only two shapes exist at all: one-for-one and two-for-one. No 2-for-2, no 1-for-2. Production holds 6,635 one-for-ones and exactly zero one-for-twos.

Not a cause

Deck size

The served deck is capped at 60 cards and typically fills 25–31. The cap is not what’s binding — supply is. Raising the cap alone would just show more re-runs.

04

A better engine is already running

This was the surprise of the review. Three engines generate cards in production and their results are shuffled into the same deck. Users have been quietly voting on them for three weeks, and the vote is lopsided.

Like rate by engine, live production decks

Every card carries a stamp saying which engine built it. These are real like/pass decisions by real users, in the same decks, on the same days.

gen_v257%
challenger31%
current18%

gen_v2 17 likes of 30 · challenger 19 of 62 · current 22 of 119. The gap between gen_v2 and the shipping engine is not a coin flip — p = 0.00009.

Why it wins

gen_v2 is the one engine that requires each side to gain on its own board, rather than requiring the viewer to win on a shared board. It also builds real packages: only 5% of its cards are one-for-ones, against 78% for the shipping engine. It is producing the trade shapes testers keep asking for.

Why to be careful

Thirty decisions is a small sample. Two-thirds of its likes come from one user, and its cards sit slightly deeper in the deck, where like rates are naturally higher. Controlling for that helps rather than hurts — in the first ten cards of a deck it runs 11 likes from 14 — but that is fourteen decisions. This is a strong lead, not a proven result.

The cheapest high-value action available right now is to give this engine more of the deck and wait two weeks. It costs no engineering work — the arm is already generating, already being served, already logging. It needs volume, not a build. If it holds at even 40%, it more than doubles the shipping engine and the entire restrictiveness debate resolves itself in its favour.

05

What to do

Ordered by value returned per unit of risk. The first group is settings changes with no code and no deploy. The second needs a small build. The third is a real decision about what the product promises.

This week — settings only
R1

Let the engine finish the league

Biggest single win

Right now it stops after six of eleven partners. Raise the stopping budget so every leaguemate gets considered. This is the one change that adds genuinely new ideas rather than reshuffling old ones — nearly half your league is currently invisible.

Change
global_target from max(30, per_opponent×6) to per_opponent × (league size − 1)
Effect
Partner coverage goes from ~6/11 to 11/11. Roughly doubles the pool of distinct ideas per league.
Cost
Generation time roughly doubles. Watch the job deadline — don’t pair this with R5 until the partner loop runs in parallel.
Risk
Low. Nothing about trade quality changes; more partners are simply examined.
R2

Weight the deck toward the engine that’s winning

Give gen_v2 a larger share of served cards and let it run two more weeks. This is the cheapest way to turn a promising 30-decision signal into a decision you can actually make.

Change
Bake-off share only — the arm is already live (bakeoff_include_gen_v2 = 1, bakeoff_serve_interleaved = 1)
Effect
A defensible sample instead of a hint. If it holds, this decides the roadmap.
Risk
Low, and self-limiting — if the like rate falls, dial it back.
R3

Loosen the “too similar” filter and the per-partner cap together

Superseded 22 Aug (later the same day): the diversity pass is a selector into 5–8 slots, not a bulk filter — loosening it buys near-duplicates and recreates #350. Withdrawn as a volume lever. See The Knockout Rules, Judged.

These two live in the same loop, and moving either one alone accomplishes nothing. Together they are the step that discards 91% of trades that already passed every quality rule.

Change
v3_diversity_max_overlap 0.4 → 0.6, with max_per_opponent 5 → 8
Effect
Real sibling variants of a strong idea survive instead of exactly one card per idea.
Risk
Medium — this is the filter that stops “the same trade plus a bench guy” five times. Don’t go past 0.6.
Trap
The per-partner cap is applied a third time by a hardcoded constant (_EXPLORATION_BASE_PER_OPP = 5) with no config knob. Without a one-line code change, raising the setting is a no-op — and you’ll conclude wrongly that the knob doesn’t matter.
R4

Lower the filler floor — one half of it, on its own

Superseded 22 Aug (later the same day): this had the two halves backwards. 91% of #141’s kills carry a sub-450 body; the 450 floor stays. The half taxing consolidation is the relative bar (filler_min_frac) — lower that instead. See The Knockout Rules, Judged.

The no-junk rule has two floors: a relative one (every extra piece must be worth 25% of the headliner) and a flat one (450 points, about a league-average starter). Move the flat floor only. The relative floor is what actually stops padded packages and has been measured to make things worse when removed.

Change
asset_floor_abs 450 → 275. Leave filler_min_frac at 0.25.
Effect
Useful bench pieces can make a three-for-one’s arithmetic work again.
Risk
Medium. Deleting this rule entirely was measured to push one-sided offers from 87% to 100%. Move one half, measure, then decide.
Pairs with
The tier-jump rule (trade_elo_gap_max). It blocks nothing today only because this rule fires first — expect it to become the new wall.
Next — small builds
R5

Allow the three-for-one

Cheapest structural fix

One comparison forbids sides differing by more than one player, which makes the single most-wanted dynasty shape — three good pieces for one stud — impossible to generate. Changing > 1 to > 2 unlocks it and its mirror.

Cost
+6% search time. Measured, not estimated.
Caveat
New 3-for-1s land straight into the filler rule’s teeth. Ship it with R4 or most of them will die on arrival.
Do it as
A knob (v3_shape_max_delta, default 1) so it’s revertible without a deploy.
R6

Give the 85% path more than two trade shapes

When your leaguemate hasn’t ranked players — most of the deck — the engine can only build one-for-one and two-for-one, and it takes the first few that pass rather than the best. Add the missing shapes and score the full set before choosing.

Effect
Roughly 12× the search space on the path that produces most of your cards, and the deck stops being “the first five things that passed.”
Risk
Medium — it rewrites the highest-traffic code in the engine. Build it behind a knob.
R7

Make the shortlist see roster fit, not just disagreement

The twelve-asset shortlist picks the players you and your partner disagree about most. But the thing that decides whether a trade is good is roster fit — how much each player is worth to that specific team. The shortlist is selecting on a different axis from the one the model optimises.

The trade this can never find: you both price Nacua at 6,200 and Bijan at 6,000, but you’re four deep at receiver and one deep at back, and your partner is the mirror. Both teams gain hundreds of points. Neither player makes either shortlist, because you don’t disagree about them. This trade is never enumerated, so it appears in no rejection log anywhere — which is exactly why previous audits couldn’t see it.

Change
Keep the top 12 by disagreement, add the top 4 by roster-fit asymmetry. Both numbers are already computed.
Cost
Up to 2.4× per pairing, measured. Less in practice, since the two lists overlap.
Do it as
A proper bake-off arm, not a settings flip. This changes what kind of trade exists.
R8

Stop letting a calibration quirk decide which way picks move

Draft picks get the same value on every board by design, so they carry zero “disagreement” — which means on any given pairing, picks either all crowd into the give pool or all get pushed out of it, decided by a board-scale artifact that has nothing to do with what either manager thinks of picks. Reserve two shortlist slots per side for picks and the direction stops being accidental.

Cost
Zero — the shortlist stays twelve.
Related
Only six picks per team enter the pool at all (picks_pool_cap = 6), roughly half of a team’s draft capital.
The real decision
R9

Price both sides against their own roster, not one shared list

Fixes the root cause

This is the only change that makes “good for both teams” a computable question on the 85% path. A player’s market value is one number for everyone — but his value to a roster is not. Your RB3 on a stacked backfield is worth a bench slot to you and a starting slot to the manager with a hole. Price each side that way and the two gains stop being mirror images.

The machinery already exists and is already trusted — it’s exactly the math the engine uses on the 15% path where both managers have ranked. This applies it to the other 85%. And it’s the most orthodox idea in the sport: replacement level and starter scarcity underpin every ranking system in fantasy football. It also directly answers feedback #304.

Effect
Mutual gain becomes a real test instead of a substitute rule. The displayed value bar doesn’t change — fairness stays on market values.
Risk
Needs its own calibration; roster-relative numbers run much smaller than market ones. Keep the market fairness ratio as the backstop so a trade can’t be “mutually marginal” while being wildly lopsided on paper.
R10

Turn on the contention-window blend

A contender and a rebuilder genuinely price a 29-year-old differently, and that difference is one of the most reliable sources of a trade both managers want. The engine has this built, wired and tested — and switched off. Today window and age only steer labels, never values, so they can reorder your deck but can never add a trade to it.

Change
Flag trade.outlook_blend
Risk
Real: for most partners the window is inferred, so a wrong guess would now move value rather than just sort order. Feedback #365 says the inference is already misreading an all-in team as a rebuilder. Fix the window signal first.
R11

Decide whether the app is allowed to suggest a trade you lose slightly

Today, on the 85% path, every suggested trade must favour the viewer. Dropping that requirement while raising the fairness floor to 0.75 was measured to double the deck, with the trade quality statistically indistinguishable and a hard cap on damage: no card could ever ask either side to give up more than 25% more than it gets.

Be clear-eyed: about 61% of the resulting cards ask the viewer to pay something. That is a defensible product — “market-even trades your leaguemate might actually accept” — but it is a different promise from today’s, and it needs card copy that says so. A gentler middle setting exists at roughly +18% deck with 31% viewer-pays.

Correction
The doubling figure is quoted in the existing audit docs, but the knob as built only delivers the +18% version — one of the two viewer-must-win checks isn’t governed by it. Reaching the doubled deck needs a small code change first. Don’t ship expecting 2×.
Decision
Yours, not the model’s. This is a product promise, not a tuning value.
06

What not to loosen

The goal is more good offers, not more offers. These rules are the reason the app doesn’t embarrass users, and one of them is the promise itself.

  • Both teams must gain something. This is “benefits both teams” written as code, and it’s the single purest rule in the system — 97% of what it blocks, nothing else objects to. Everything it removes is a trade where one side gains essentially nothing. Once volume is restored elsewhere, this is the rule worth tightening.
  • Both teams can still field a lineup. Not negotiable, and currently too permissive rather than too strict — it ignores FLEX and bench depth entirely.
  • The relative filler floor. The flat 450 floor is worth moving; the “25% of the headliner” rule is not. Deleting the filler rule was measured to push one-sided offers from 87% to 100%.
  • The fairness floor on the no-board path. Today it’s a taste setting. The moment you ship R11 it becomes the only safety rail — it is the cap on how badly either manager can be asked to lose.
  • Never re-show a declined or pending trade. Feedback #370 says this is already leaking. That’s a credibility cost with no upside.
  • The pick-churn ban. Nearly free, and it stops the “a 1st for a 1st riding along inside a bigger deal” shape testers complained about.

One warning about sequencing. Because the rules overlap so heavily, loosening them one at a time and measuring will mostly show nothing — another rule catches the same trade. Four rules all punish “one big player for several smaller ones,” and 97.6% of rejections are made redundantly. Change them in the paired groups above, or you will correctly measure “no effect” and draw the wrong conclusion from it.

07

How much to trust this

Solid

  • Everything in section 2 is a direct count over all 10,560 served cards in the live database.
  • The six-partner ceiling is confirmed twice: predicted from the code, then found in the data as a sharp spike at exactly six with a cliff at five cards per partner.
  • All settings values were read from live production, not from code defaults.

Shakier

  • Five users, six leagues. Mostly your own testing. Treat everything as directional.
  • The engine-comparison result rests on 30 decisions, two-thirds from one user.
  • Every rejection count comes from replaying one league — the only one with enough ranked members. Ratios hold; absolute deck-size forecasts don’t.
  • Partner attribution assumes rosters haven’t changed since the card was served, so partner coverage is slightly noisy. The spike at six is far too sharp to be noise.

Three things the existing audit docs get wrong

  • The bake-off is live, not dark. Prior reports state that alternative engines generate but are never served, and therefore have zero user decisions. In production bakeoff_serve_interleaved = 1: three engines’ cards are interleaved into real decks and have collected 211 real decisions. That data is the most valuable signal in this report and nobody was reading it.
  • The “doubles the deck” figure is not what the switch delivers. The knob only governs one of the two viewer-must-win checks; as built it delivers roughly +18%, not +112%.
  • Line references in the 2026-08-19 reviews have drifted by several hundred lines. Anyone working from them should re-locate against the current code.
