01

The short answer

The first report’s diagnosis is right. Its prescription is incomplete, and one of its success metrics is pointing the wrong way.

1. The six-partner ceiling is worse than reported. It isn’t that each deck reaches six of eleven leaguemates. In a league where only you have ranked players, it’s the same six every time — the code visits partners in roster order and stops. Across 6 to 11 refreshes, 5 of 13 and 7 of 13 leaguemates never appeared in any deck, ever. Those managers are invisible to you, permanently, for zero good reason.

2. “Like rate” measures whether the viewer wins, not whether both teams do. Cards built where the viewer wins by construction are liked 30% of the time. Cards that genuinely require the other manager to gain too are liked 19%. If you tune toward likes, you will tune toward lopsided offers — the opposite of what you asked for.

3. The scoreboard that matters reads almost zero. Four hundred sixty-five likes have produced fifteen mutual matches and three accepted trades, ever. Of 127 real Sleeper trades the app has captured, exactly one partially resembled anything it suggested. That is the number to move.

The gen_v2 finding survives every confound this review threw at it, and sharpens: on the only path where it runs, the shipping engine is liked 6% of the time and gen_v2 57%.

The mechanism is at trade_service.py:5093: partners are sorted ranked-first, then roster order, and the sweep breaks at the card budget. In a league with six boards the break lands in different places and coverage rotates. In a league where only you have ranked, nothing reorders — the same tail is skipped every single time. This is the single cheapest fix in either report: rotate where the sweep starts.

Like rate by how the card was built

“Consensus” cards are built when the partner hasn’t ranked — the viewer wins by construction. “Divergence” cards require both sides to gain on their own boards.

Two things to read here. The shipping engine’s divergence path — the one designed for mutual gain — is the worst-performing thing in the product (2 likes of 33). gen_v2 replaces exactly that path. And the challenger’s consensus cards, the arm that lets the viewer lose slightly, are liked 45% against the current engine’s 23% — early evidence (n=20) that users don’t punish an honest, market-even offer. That bears directly on the “real decision” the first report left with you.

Same shapes, opposite results

The first report wondered whether gen_v2 wins because users simply like multi-player packages. No: the current engine’s own multi-asset cards are liked 2 of 35; gen_v2’s are liked 16 of 27. It builds better packages, not just more of them.

A claim I got wrong, then checked

The first draft of this page called calc_opened a dead instrument because it averaged 0.00. It isn’t dead — it’s rare. The bit is set on 3 of 525 recorded outcomes, and the “open in calculator” tap has fired 7 times, ever. The 0.00 was two-decimal rounding. The real gap is small: 4 of those 7 taps never reached an outcome row, because the bit only rides on a later like/pass of the same card, and someone who opens the calculator usually sends from there instead.

04

What changes in the recommendations

Nothing here contradicts the first report’s R3–R10. Two things get added or reframed, one is retracted.

R0

Rotate the partner sweep — do this before R1

New · zero cost

R1 (“let the engine finish the league”) doubles generation time, and the logs say p90 is already 5.3 seconds. Most of R1’s value can be had for nothing: in leagues where partner order never changes, start the sweep at a different leaguemate each refresh. The deck still stops at six partners — but a different six, so over a week every manager gets considered. Fixes the permanent blind spot without touching the latency budget.

Change
Seed the order of unranked members per deck (e.g. rotate by job count, or a per-deck shuffle seeded on user+league+week) at trade_service.py:5093. Ranked-first stays.
Effect
Union coverage over a week goes from 6–8 of 13 to 13 of 13 in single-board leagues. Per-deck count unchanged.
Risk
Negligible. Same rules, same budget, different visiting order.
R2′

Judge gen_v2 by matches, not likes

Reframed

Weighting the deck toward gen_v2 is still the right call. But grade it on the two-sided signals: mutual-match rate and partner-side like rate (when the partner sees the mirror card, do they like it?). A like rate win can be bought by favouring the viewer; a match can’t. The first report’s success criterion (“holds at 40% likes”) would pass an engine that had learned to fleece.

Baseline to beat
3.2% of likes become matches. Any arm that doubles that has proven something no like rate can.
R11′

The “viewer may lose slightly” decision now has early evidence

Reframed

The first report left this as a pure product call. The challenger arm is that product, and its consensus cards are liked 45% against the current engine’s 23% on the same basis. Twenty decisions is thin, but the direction says users read a market-even offer as a better offer, not a worse one. Let the challenger earn the rest of the sample alongside gen_v2 rather than deciding in the abstract.

R12

Withdrawn: the calculator-open event is not broken

Retracted

Recorded 3 times against 7 taps in four weeks. The event works; the action is rare. If the 4 lost taps matter, the fix is one line — write the outcome row at tap time instead of waiting for a swipe — but it is not the instrument failure I claimed, and it should not be first in line for anything.

Drop “#370 is leaking” from the keep-list

Corrected

The first report listed re-serving declined trades as an active leak. 151 of the 177 cases are your 17 Aug amnesty working as designed. The residual is two liked trades re-shown within their 7-day window — worth a look, not worth a rule.

How much to trust this

Solid

  • The blind-spot table is a direct union over every served card per user-and-league. The single-board leagues show 5 and 7 never-seen partners across 11 and 6 decks; that is not a sampling artefact.
  • The like-rate-by-basis split is over all 621 decided cards with a recorded basis.
  • The match and real-trade counts are whole-table counts.
  • All seven code claims were read at the cited lines in the current tree.

Shakier

  • Within-deck arm comparison rests on 11 current-engine decisions. Rank-matched on 34 vs 16. Basis-matched on 33 vs 30.
  • The challenger’s 45% is 9 of 20.
  • Real-trade matching only runs for leagues with telemetry on, with a 14-day lookback — “1 of 127” is a floor on misses, not a ceiling.
  • Still five users. Still mostly you.
