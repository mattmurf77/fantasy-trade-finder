# Round 2 / Topic 04 — Matching in Closed, Repeated-Game Communities + Fantasy-Domain Analogs

> **Research memo, 2026-08-15.** Round 1 mapped matchmaking systems built for anonymous stranger markets (dating apps, marketplaces). This memo covers the flagged gap: a dynasty league is a **repeated game among ~12 acquaintances**, where every transaction is public, every counterparty is permanent, and the whole community judges every executed deal. Half A covers the social science of exchange inside closed communities; Half B covers what the fantasy-sports domain itself already does. Confidence flags: **[H]** = multiple independent quality sources, **[M]** = one solid source or consistent commentary, **[L]** = inference/thin sourcing.

## Findings — Half A: closed-community dynamics

### A1. Embeddedness: trades among acquaintances ride on the relationship, not the deal **[H]**

Granovetter's foundational 1985 argument: economic action is *embedded* in ongoing structures of social relations. Actors are neither purely rational atoms (the "undersocialized" economics view) nor norm-automatons (the "oversocialized" sociology view) — they transact through concrete personal relationships, and **trust generated by those relationships is what makes transactions possible at all**. Two corollaries matter for FTF:

- Repeated relations *reduce* transaction costs (you don't need contracts or vetting with someone you've dealt with for 5 seasons), which is why embedded pairs trade more with each other — the NBA-GM finding in B4 is the same phenomenon.
- The same embeddedness *raises the stakes of malfeasance*: cheating a network member is punished through the whole network (gossip, reputation loss, freeze-out), not just by the victim. In a 12-person league, "fleecing" someone is broadcast to all 11 others instantly.

### A2. The shadow of the future: dynasty leagues are near-infinite repeated games **[H]**

Axelrod's *Evolution of Cooperation* (with the "shadow of the future" concept from Shubik): when the probability of future interaction is high enough, conditional cooperation beats defection even where one-shot logic says exploit. Experimental economics (Dal Bó 2005, infinitely repeated PD experiments; child-development replications) confirms the effect empirically — opportunistic behavior drops sharply when players know they'll meet again.

A dynasty league is about the strongest shadow-of-the-future environment imaginable: fixed membership, multi-year horizon, year-round contact, full observability of everyone's moves. Implications:

- **One-shot surplus extraction is negative-EV.** A manager who "wins" a lopsided trade gains value once and loses counterparties for years. Domain commentary (B5) confirms this exact dynamic: send bad offers repeatedly and "leaguemates will be conditioned to not take them seriously."
- **Cooperation is the equilibrium the tool should reinforce**, not a nicety. A trade engine that maximizes one side's gain is optimizing against the game's actual payoff structure.

### A3. Friends negotiate *worse* than strangers — and often avoid negotiating at all **[H]**

This is the single most counterintuitive, load-bearing finding of Half A. Harvard PON's research summaries: teams of friends reach **fewer integrative (win-win) agreements** and less accurate judgments than teams of non-friends. Mechanisms:

- **Relational accommodation:** friends default to equal splits and premature compromise to protect the relationship, leaving joint value on the table (PON; also the "relational self-construal" literature — dyads with high relational goals produce inefficient economic outcomes but high relational capital).
- **Asymmetric self-sacrifice:** in a 2006 study, people *selling* to a friend asked for less than the friend was willing to pay; buyers did not correspondingly overpay. Communal norms make it embarrassing to claim value from a friend.
- **Communal vs exchange norms (Ramírez-Fernández et al. 2019, NCMR):** close relationships carry *communal* norms (responsiveness to needs, no scorekeeping); market deals carry *exchange* norms (tit-for-tat, ledger-keeping). Buyers expect more favorable offers from best friends than from acquaintances — so a friend who makes a market-rate offer **violates an expectation and causes offense** even though the offer is objectively fair.
- **Avoidance:** because impasse or hard bargaining might damage the relationship, friends frequently prefer *not transacting at all* to negotiating. PON explicitly recommends structural workarounds (objective standards, third-party valuations, agreed procedures) when friends must deal.

This is a direct explanation for the dead-league problem that has nothing to do with laziness or valuation disagreement: **in acquaintance markets, the socially safest move is silence.** Every un-sent offer is avoided relational risk. A trade tool's core job in this market is not finding value — it is *lowering the relational cost of initiating*.

### A4. Fairness, envy, and the league-as-third-party-judge **[H]**

Fehr & Schmidt's inequity-aversion model (QJE 1999): people sacrifice material payoff to resist inequitable outcomes, and **disadvantageous inequity (envy — "he got more than me") is weighted roughly twice as heavily as advantageous inequity (guilt)**. Third-party punishment research extends this: bystanders who suffered nothing from a violation will *pay a personal cost* to punish perceived unfairness, and a small fraction of inequity-averse punishers is enough to discipline a whole group's behavior.

Map to a league: every executed trade is judged by 10 non-parties who are also competitors — they are *materially affected* third parties (a rival getting stronger hurts them), which makes them stronger-than-lab punishers. The veto is institutionalized third-party punishment; the league-chat pile-on is its informal twin. Fantasy-domain commentary (B3) confirms both forms are live. Design consequence: **a trade suggestion must be defensible to the eleven, not just agreeable to the two.** The relevant fairness standard is not "both parties gained" (the two-board test) but "no observer can credibly call it a fleece or collusion."

### A5. Indirect speech and plausible deniability: how acquaintances make risky offers **[H]**

Pinker, Nowak & Lee (PNAS 2008, "The logic of indirect speech") formalize why people say "would you be open to…" instead of "give me X for Y": an off-record, indirect proposal lets a cooperative hearer accept while denying an antagonistic hearer the grounds to take offense or retaliate. Crucially, the follow-up work (Lee & Pinker, "strategic speaker") shows indirectness retains value **even when both parties privately know what's being asked** — because it prevents *common knowledge*: without an on-record proposal, there is nothing to gossip about, no rejection event that both sides know the other knows about. Relationship-negotiation costs (who dominates, are we communal or transactional) are exactly what a blunt offer between friends risks disturbing.

Real-world GM behavior (B4) is this theory operationalized: informal year-round "gauging interest" calls, courtesy norms, trusted channels — deals surface as formal proposals only after soft-sounding has established mutual interest.

**FTF's structural gift:** an algorithmic suggestion is *machine-generated indirection*. "The app surfaced this" is the ultimate plausible deniability — neither party has made an on-record request, so a pass creates no rejection event and no common knowledge. This property is fragile and must be protected deliberately (see Transfer notes T1).

### A6. What keeps small closed exchange economies alive — and what kills them **[H]**

**Capitol Hill Babysitting Co-op** (Sweeney & Sweeney 1977; popularized by Krugman): ~150 families exchanging babysitting via scrip. When scrip per family fell too low, families **hoarded** (everyone wanted a buffer of scrip before spending, so everyone offered to sit and nobody went out) — a demand-shortage recession in a toy economy. Fixed by issuing more scrip (new members got 30 hours' worth, owed back only 20); later over-issuance produced the opposite failure (too much scrip, nobody willing to sit — inflation). Non-Keynesian critiques (Mises Institute; "Between the Balance Sheets") add governance detail: the co-op also used rule changes (mandatory minimum going-out) and price-fixing of scrip contributed to both failure modes. Lessons regardless of macro-school: **closed markets stall when participants hoard the medium of exchange as a precautionary buffer, and liquidity is a managed variable, not a given.** Dynasty analog: draft picks are the league's scrip; pick-hoarding managers and "I'm saving my assets for the right deal" postures are the hoarding equilibrium; trade droughts are the recession.

**LETS (Local Exchange Trading Systems):** hundreds of community barter-currency schemes peaked mid-1990s, then sharply declined. Documented failure modes (Aldridge & Patterson "LETS get real"; Australian appraisals; Wikipedia synthesis):

1. **Failure to reach critical mass** — small systems have large mismatches between what's offered and what's wanted, so trade-per-member stays trivially low and members drift away.
2. **Organizer burnout** — schemes run on one or two volunteers who exhaust themselves; most schemes "stall and die" this way. (Direct analog: the commissioner carries dynasty-league health, and commissioner burnout kills leagues.)
3. **Ideological members who don't actually want to trade much** — participation motivated by community values rather than exchange keeps volume permanently low.

A 12-team league is *below LETS critical mass by construction* — the offered/wanted mismatch problem is the default state. The countermeasures available are exactly what a trade engine does: search the full combinatorial space so no feasible match is missed, and widen the match set (multi-asset packages, picks as the fungible bridge asset).

### A7. Reputation systems: thick implicit reputation ≠ explicit ratings **[H]**

Resnick et al.'s eBay work and Tadelis's Annual Review: reputation/feedback systems demonstrably create trust among *strangers* (established seller IDs fetch measurably higher prices in controlled experiments), but suffer chronic **ratings inflation and feedback underprovision** — and in *bilateral* rating settings, **fear of retaliation** drives dishonest positives (eBay eventually removed sellers' ability to negatively rate buyers). In a 12-person league, everyone already carries a thick, multi-year *implicit* reputation ledger — the information problem explicit ratings solve does not exist, while the retaliation problem they create is maximal (you will face your rater in the playoffs). Conclusion: **do not build visible reputation scores inside a closed community**; use behavioral signals (responsiveness, acceptance patterns) privately, if at all.

### A8. Rejection design in non-anonymous networks: ignore must be a first-class, invisible action **[M]**

LinkedIn deliberately never notifies a user that a connection request was declined; "Ignore" is a first-class action and even an explicit decline is invisible to the sender, who sees only a still-pending request. Commentary is unanimous on the rationale: in professional (repeated-relationship) networks, a visible rejection creates permanent awkwardness, so the platform absorbs the ambiguity — the sender can always believe the request was never seen. This is the same double-opt-in/invisible-pass pattern Round 1 found in dating apps, but the *reason* differs: dating apps hide passes to protect strangers' feelings at scale; professional networks hide them because **the two parties will keep meeting**. FTF's existing silent rejection is the correct pattern per both literatures; the non-anonymous rationale is the binding one.

## Findings — Half B: fantasy/sports domain analogs

### B1. KeepTradeCut: the market's crowdsourced price oracle **[H]**

Mechanics (from KTC's own FAQ):

- **The loop:** users answer forced-choice ordinal questions — given 3 players, tag one Keep / one Trade / one Cut. Each answer yields three pairwise preferences. An "adapted Elo algorithm" (proprietary) converts the pairwise stream into cardinal values distributed across a value spectrum.
- **The flywheel:** users must periodically submit a KTC vote to keep viewing rankings — the data collection is the paywall. Rankings update on every submission, so values are continuously fresh (recency handled by flow, not by explicit decay — new votes simply overwhelm old signal for players in the news). **[M]** on the internals; they call it "secret sauce."
- **Manipulation resistance:** occasional "test" KTCs with one obviously correct answer catch inattentive or bad-faith voters; multiple unspecified quality-control measures beyond that. Community criticism persists that KTC values run hot on hype players and that first-round rookie picks are systematically overvalued relative to their bust rates, and that determined users can nudge thin markets. **[M]**
- **Fairness presentation:** the trade calculator shows side-by-side value totals with a balance meter, and applies a **"Value Adjustment"** that adds value to the side receiving fewer/better players — explicitly to prevent "12 third-round picks for DeAndre Hopkins" from scoring as fair. This is a quantity-for-quality correction encoding roster-spot scarcity and stud premium — the domain's institutionalized answer to package-vs-stud fairness.

The deeper finding: **KTC became the league's shared fairness language.** "KTC says it's even" is how acquaintances make exchange-norm offers without violating communal norms (A3) — the neutral oracle takes the social blame for hard bargaining. Cost: calculator-anchored negotiation ("both sides demand a calc win") adds a new friction; a deal that both boards love can still die because one side loses the public scoreboard. **[M]**

### B2. The existing trade-suggestion tool landscape **[H]**

- **DLF Trade Finder** — *not* a suggester: a searchable database of real executed trades harvested nightly from thousands of MyFantasyLeague dynasty leagues, filterable by league size/scoring/lineup, with time-of-trade vs current-value comparison. Its fairness epistemology is **precedent** ("comparable sales"), not model output. Users are told to use it to *build* offers grounded in what players actually fetched.
- **Dynasty Nerds DynastyGM Trade Finder** — the closest true analog to FTF: syncs the league (Sleeper, MFL, Fleaflicker, FFPC), surfaces trade opportunities based on your roster, other rosters, and current values, explicitly framed as deals "fair enough that the other owner is likely to accept." Paywalled (free tier: 3 preview trades/day). No evidence found that it models the *counterparty's* preferences beyond values — suggestions appear value-balance + positional-need driven. **[M]**
- **Dynasty Daddy Trade Finder** — exists (trade-finder route on the site); depth unverified (page is a JS app; fetch returned no content). **[L]**
- **FantasyCalc / "Fantasy Player Trade Values"** — values generated automatically from millions of real league trades; same precedent-based philosophy as DLF, delivered as a value chart. **[M]**
- **Sleeper (the host platform)** — two socially significant mechanics: (1) the **trade block**: posting players/picks you'd move auto-announces to league chat as a conversation starter — a *broadcast soft-sounding* mechanic, the platform's institutional "would you be open to…"; (2) frictionless trade initiation from any surface (roster, player page, matchup). Declines of formal offers ARE visible to the sender on host platforms — which is precisely why the informal layers (trade block, DMs) carry the early-stage risk instead. **[H]**

**Gap in the market:** every mainstream tool answers "is this trade fair?" or "what have others paid?"; only DynastyGM (paywalled, value-driven) attempts "whom should I trade with and for what?", and **nothing found models the social/relational layer at all** — no tool knows who responds to offers, who holds grudges, who hoards, or what the league will tolerate. **[M]**

### B3. Trade culture: why offers die, why leagues die, and how the league judges **[H]**

Converging domain commentary (Footballguys' Ultimate Guide to Trades, Fantasy Footballers etiquette guide, DLF forums, Apex, CBS mailbags):

- **Offer-response norms are sacred.** The #1 cited etiquette violation is silence: "nothing is worse than sending a trade offer and the other owner acting like it never happened." Non-response reads as disrespect and kills future dialogue. Corollary: tools that make declining cheap and fast protect the social fabric.
- **Lowballs are reputation spend.** Repeated lowball/spam offers condition the league to ignore you; a manager's *offer history* is their credit rating. Mass-blasting identical offers reads as insincere.
- **Talk first, propose second.** Guides consistently recommend opening a conversation (DM, league chat, trade block) before sending a formal offer — the domain has organically rediscovered A5's soft-sounding protocol.
- **Private negotiation is the rule.** "Never blast a league mate in league chat" over an offer; airing negotiations is a relationship-ending move. (Note the tension: the trade block is *deliberately* public, but it broadcasts *availability*, never *the offer*.)
- **Values legitimately differ** — pick-vs-player and win-now-vs-rebuild orientations mean an offer fair on one board is a decline on another; guides tell managers to ask "what are they working toward?" — i.e., model the counterparty's plan, not just their roster.
- **Why leagues die:** abandoment cascades — a couple of checked-out managers → fewer viable counterparties → less activity → more checkouts. Commissioner countermeasures: offseason programming (the "NFL offseason is the dynasty regular season"), taxi-squad poaching mechanics, dispersal drafts for orphan teams, replacing inactive managers quickly, and prize-pool allocations for engagement. Activity is treated as a *managed* league resource — the babysitting co-op's liquidity lesson, rediscovered. **[H]**
- **Veto culture:** near-consensus among commentators that vetoes are for provable collusion only, never mere lopsidedness ("owners are not responsible for making sure other managers make trades they like"). Yet in practice leagues do veto lopsided deals, and veto fights are league-defining drama — Old Man Dynasty's "the trade veto is the most fun you can have without winning your league" documents a veto of a 5-superstar consolidation deal on *competitive-balance* grounds, celebrated as community lore. Both things are true: vetoes are officially collusion-only and unofficially a fairness referendum. A suggestion engine must survive the unofficial standard. **[H]**

### B4. Real-world GM norms: the professionalized version of acquaintance trading **[M/H]**

HoopsHype's executive interviews and NBA front-office commentary describe a mature repeated-game trade market among ~30 permanent counterparties:

- **Relationships route deal flow.** GMs preferentially call trusted counterparts (to prevent leaks and "foul play"), which is why the same franchise pairs trade repeatedly. Trust literally determines the feasible match set.
- **Reputation gates access.** Teams/agents known to leak get frozen out of discussions except when unavoidable. One organization that mass-texts trade probes is regarded as an outlier and mildly disreputable — offer-spam is low-status even at the pro level.
- **Soft-sounding precedes proposals.** Year-round informal conversations ("gauging interest," courtesy calls, keeping relationships warm) mean deadline deals are "picking up a previous conversation rather than starting a new one." Formal proposals arrive only after mutual interest is established informally.
- **Selective shopping protects value.** Calling every team signals desperation and devalues the asset — breadth of solicitation is itself information.

The transferable core: in permanent-membership markets, **the informal pre-offer layer is where most of the negotiation actually happens**, and the formal offer is a confirmation step. Tools that force acquaintances to skip straight to formal offers are fighting the market's native protocol.

## 1. Best practices

1. **Double opt-in before any identity/intent reveal** — the suggestion must remain "the app's idea" until *both* sides have independently expressed interest; a pass must generate zero common knowledge (A5, A8, Round-1 dating-app convergence). **[H]**
2. **Make declining effortless, guilt-free, and invisible** — one-tap pass, no notification to anyone, no "seen" receipts, no lingering pending state visible to the other side (A8, B3 response-norms). **[H]**
3. **Anchor every suggestion to a neutral, league-legible fairness standard** — a consensus-value oracle (KTC-style) and/or precedent ("similar trades executed in N real leagues") lets both parties bargain hard while blaming the oracle, converting communal-norm friction into exchange-norm safety (A3, B1, B2). **[H]**
4. **Design for the eleven, not just the two:** suggested trades should pass a third-party defensibility test (no observer could credibly cry fleece/collusion) — bounded value gap, articulable two-sided rationale, quantity-for-quality adjustment à la KTC's Value Adjustment (A4, B1, B3-veto). **[H]**
5. **Optimize for repeat business, not per-trade surplus** — the engine's objective in a repeated game is maximizing *executed trades over seasons*, which means mutual-gain suggestions with modest, defensible asymmetries; a "win" that poisons a counterparty relationship is a net loss (A1, A2, B3). **[H]**
6. **Provide a soft-sounding layer before the formal offer** — trade-block-style availability broadcasting, "would you move X?" probes, or the suggestion itself acting as the icebreaker; the formal proposal should be the *confirmation* of interest already established (A5, B3, B4). **[H]**
7. **Treat league liquidity as a managed variable** — monitor trade volume, detect droughts and hoarding (pick-stockpiling, non-responders), and inject liquidity deliberately: offseason suggestion pushes, themed events, wider match sets in dry spells (A6, B3). **[M]**
8. **Ration and quality-gate outgoing suggestions** — every offer sent in a user's name spends their league reputation; low volume + high quality beats spray-and-pray, which is low-status from Sleeper leagues to NBA front offices (B3, B4). **[H]**
9. **Ground fairness in precedent where possible** — "teams in 40 similar leagues made this trade" is more socially persuasive than any model score, because comparable sales are how humans justify prices to third parties (B2-DLF/FantasyCalc). **[M]**
10. **Model the counterparty's *plan*, not just their roster** — contender/rebuilder window, positional intent, pick appetite; fairness on values with wrongness on direction is the most common legitimate decline (B3, Matt's own trade philosophy: roster fit first). **[M]**
11. **Keep negotiations private, availability public** — the only broadcast-safe artifact is "what I'd move" (trade block); offers, counters, and passes are strictly bilateral (B3). **[H]**

## 2. Antipatterns

1. **Revealing rejection** — any signal that lets a sender learn (or infer) they were passed on: read receipts, "declined" states, timing tells, or suggestion-provenance leaks ("Matt was shown this trade"). One leaked pass converts the app from safe intermediary to embarrassment machine (A5, A8). **[H]**
2. **Fleece-maximizing suggestions** — surfacing trades where one side clearly wins. The winner's gain is one-shot; the damage (grudge, league gossip, veto, distrust of the app) compounds. Also the fastest way to make the *app itself* the league villain (A2, A4, B3). **[H]**
3. **Explicit reputation scores visible inside the league** — star ratings, "trade partner grades," responsiveness leaderboards. In a 12-person permanent group these create retaliation loops and formalize grudges; eBay had to dismantle bilateral ratings even among strangers (A7). **[H]**
4. **Offer spam / suggestion spam** — high-volume, low-signal suggestions train the league to ignore both the sender and the app (B3, B4; LETS's low-trade-per-member death spiral). **[H]**
5. **Making the calculator the only judge** — pure value-balance framing invites scoreboard-brained negotiation where every deal must "win the calc" for both sides simultaneously (impossible), and hype-inflated crowd values propagate (B1 criticisms). Present values as *ranges/defensibility*, not verdicts. **[M]**
6. **Ignoring the third-party audience** — treating a trade as done when two parties agree; the league-chat verdict and veto window are part of the transaction. A tool that regularly produces "vetoed or ridiculed" trades dies socially (A4, B3). **[H]**
7. **Forcing formal offers cold** — UX that jumps straight from suggestion to a binding on-platform offer with no informal probe step recreates exactly the relational risk that keeps acquaintances from trading (A3, A5, B4). **[M]**
8. **Letting hoarders and ghosts poison the pool silently** — suggesting trades into non-responsive managers burns senders' goodwill and teaches users the app doesn't understand their league; the babysitting co-op stalled on exactly this precautionary-hoarding equilibrium (A6, B3). **[M]**
9. **Equal-split fairness theater** — friends already over-compromise (A3); a tool that pushes perfectly 50/50 deals amplifies relational accommodation and leaves integrative value (window/need complementarity) unexploited. The best trades are *unequal on values and superior on both boards*. **[M]**

## 3. What matters most (ranked)

1. **Protecting plausible deniability end-to-end.** The app's entire social value proposition is that a suggestion is nobody's request and a pass is nobody's rejection. This is the crown jewel and it is binary — one design leak destroys it. (A5, A8) **[H]**
2. **Third-party defensibility of executed trades.** The league judges every deal; the app's long-run reputation inside a league equals the worst trade it ever brokered. Bounded asymmetry + articulable rationale + consensus anchor. (A4, B1, B3) **[H]**
3. **Lowering initiation cost.** The binding constraint on league liquidity is un-sent offers (relational risk aversion), not un-found matches. Every design choice should be scored on "does this make starting a conversation cheaper?" (A3, B4) **[H]**
4. **Repeat-game objective function.** Optimize executed-trades-per-season-per-pair, not per-trade value capture. This single objective choice drives most other rules (fleece bans, spam rationing, mutual-gain framing). (A2) **[H]**
5. **A shared fairness language.** Some neutral oracle — crowd values, precedent, or FTF's own Elo boards — that both parties and the league accept as the blame-absorbing standard. Which oracle matters less than that it is *legible and external to both parties*. (B1, B2) **[H]**
6. **Liquidity management as a product feature.** Droughts, hoarding, and ghost managers are detectable and partially treatable; league health is retention. (A6, B3) **[M]**
7. **The informal pre-offer layer.** Soft-sounding mechanics (availability broadcasting, interest probes) do the heavy lifting before any formal proposal. (A5, B3, B4) **[M]**

## 4. What doesn't matter even though it seems like it should

- **Stranger-market trust infrastructure** — ratings, verification, escrow, fraud detection. The trust problem those solve doesn't exist among league mates (identity and history are already common knowledge); importing them adds retaliation surfaces without information gain (A7). **[H]**
- **Matching-algorithm scale and sophistication.** Round-1 systems fight million-user search problems; a 12-team league's pairwise space is 66 pairs and the full package-combinatorics is trivially searchable. The bottleneck is social willingness, not computational discovery. Marginal algorithm IQ buys almost nothing past "finds all mutually beneficial packages"; marginal social-safety design buys everything. **[H]**
- **Cardinal value precision.** Whether a player is worth 5,400 or 5,150 points is irrelevant; what matters is *ordinal defensibility* (would a neutral league mate call this range fair?). KTC's dominance despite known value inflation proves legibility beats accuracy (B1). **[M]**
- **Veto-proofing mechanics.** Vetoes are rare, commissioner-owned, and — per Old Man Dynasty — sometimes community-strengthening drama. Building veto-avoidance *features* (beyond suggesting defensible trades in the first place) solves a corner case; and note vetoes mostly punish *lopsidedness*, which the engine should never produce anyway (B3). **[M]**
- **Anonymity options.** It's tempting to offer "anonymous offers" inside a league; in a 12-person market the counterparty set is so small that anonymity is fake (roster reveals sender) and attempting it signals bad faith. Deniability ≠ anonymity: the working pattern is *attributable availability, deniable interest* (A5, B3). **[M]**
- **Getting managers to respond faster via pressure** — nudges shaming non-responders ("Matt has seen your offer") violate rule #1 and formalize the exact awkwardness the app exists to absorb. Response-rate problems are better treated by routing suggestions toward responsive managers (A8, B3). **[M]**

## Transfer notes for FTF

**T1 — The suggestion IS the indirection; guard it like a secret.** FTF's suggestion replaces the "would you be open to…" call. Concretely: (a) never show either party whether the other saw/passed a suggestion; (b) only reveal mutual interest after both independently engage (double opt-in — Round-1 convergence, now with the stronger repeated-game rationale); (c) when a match *is* revealed, frame provenance symmetrically ("FTF found a fit between your boards"), never "X wants your player" — that's an on-record request X never made. Watch timing side-channels (an offer arriving minutes after a user acts in-app leaks intent).

**T2 — Add a "league-defensible" gate to the trade engine.** Beyond the existing dual-board mutual-gain check (`user_gain_epsilon` covers the two parties), add a third check approximating the neutral observer: value gap within a bounded band on a *public* consensus anchor, KTC-style quantity-for-quality adjustment so N-for-1 packages don't score as fair, and a machine-written two-sided rationale ("You get win-now RB help; they get the 2027 1st their rebuild wants") attached to every suggestion. The rationale doubles as the user's league-chat defense if the deal is questioned — give them the words.

**T3 — Rename the objective in the code's head: liquidity, not surplus.** Rank candidate suggestions by P(executed) × relationship-safety, not by value edge. A suggestion with a modest, obviously-mutual gain beats a technically-larger but explainable-as-fleece one every time. (Brand note: the app is named *Fleeced* — lean into it as the thing the app *prevents*, e.g., fairness messaging like "nobody gets fleeced," because a trade-suggestion app whose output ever looks like actual fleecing will have its name quoted back at it in every league chat.)

**T4 — Build the soft-sounding layer.** Sleeper's trade block is the domain-native probe mechanic. FTF equivalents: let users flag assets "available" / "untouchable" (attributable availability), and let a suggestion's first surfaced step be a low-stakes probe ("Feel out interest?") rather than a formal proposal. Sleeper's formal-offer decline is visible to the sender — FTF's pre-offer layer should be where all deniable early-stage interest lives, handing off to Sleeper only once both sides are warm.

**T5 — Spend user reputation frugally.** Rate-limit outgoing suggestion-driven offers per counterparty pair; suppress near-duplicate suggestions; never let FTF become the tool that made its user "the spam guy." Consider routing: prioritize counterparties with a history of responding (private signal, never displayed).

**T6 — Instrument league health like the babysitting co-op treasurer.** Per-league metrics: trades/month, offer-response rate, pick-concentration (hoarding index), share of managers with zero activity in 30 days. Drought interventions: widen suggestion asymmetry tolerance slightly, push offseason suggestion moments, surface rebuild/contend complementarities. A dead league churns 12 users at once — league health *is* retention.

**T7 — Fairness presentation: ranges and precedent, not verdicts.** Show "within league-normal range" rather than a winner/loser needle; where possible cite precedent ("similar deals happen in real leagues" — DLF/FantasyCalc-style comparable sales beat model output for third-party persuasion). Avoid training FTF users to demand a calc-win on every deal.

**T8 — Don't import round-1 stranger-market machinery.** No in-league ratings, no verification theater, no anonymity features, no "seen" mechanics. The 12-person market's trust layer already exists; FTF's job is to lower the *social* cost of using it.

## 5. Not researched / follow-up topics

- **Ramírez-Fernández et al. (2019) full text** — Wiley 403'd; findings taken from abstract/secondary summaries. Worth pulling for effect sizes on friend-vs-stranger offer expectations.
- **Dynasty Nerds DynastyGM suggestion internals** — paywalled; no public detail on whether it models counterparty acceptance probability or just value balance. A hands-on teardown (subscribe for a month) would establish the true competitive bar. Same for Dynasty Daddy's trade-finder depth (JS app, unfetchable).
- **Reddit r/DynastyFF primary threads** — Reddit blocks the crawler; dead-league, KTC-manipulation, and offer-spam complaints here come via secondary commentary. A manual browse session would firm up the user-complaint taxonomy.
- **Private Discord trading communities** (sports cards, TCG, sneaker groups) — planned but unreached; their mod-enforced norms (proof-of-trade posts, middleman systems, blacklists) are the closest living "closed community trade governance" analog.
- **Nextdoor's neighbor-interaction rejection design** — thin public documentation found; deprioritized after LinkedIn provided the clean pattern.
- **Multi-team (3-way) trade mechanics** — LETS critical-mass logic suggests 3-way trades materially widen a 12-team market's feasible set; how existing platforms/leagues handle them (Sleeper doesn't natively) is unexplored.
- **Kula ring / gift-economy anthropology (Mauss, Malinowski)** — the deep literature on obligatory reciprocal exchange in closed communities; skipped for time, likely reinforces A1/A2 rather than changing conclusions.
- **Quantitative dead-league data** — no hard numbers found on dynasty league mortality rates or trade-volume distributions; FTF's own Sleeper-synced data could become the first real dataset here (research asset, and a marketing one).
